3D Printer — How a Machine Builds an Object Layer by Layer

Layers, heat, motion and flow — the four things a printer has to get right

1. Building by adding, not cutting

  • A lathe or a mill makes a shape by taking material away. A 3D printer makes one by adding it.
  • The object is sliced into flat layers, and the nozzle draws each layer on top of the last.
  • Nothing is ever carved: every millimetre of the object was placed there deliberately.

2. Layers and resolution

  • Number of layers N = height ÷ layer height. Halve the layer height and you double the layers.
  • A sloping surface comes out as a staircase, and each step is one layer high — orbit in close to the sloping wall of the part and you can count them.
  • So thin layers look smoother but take longer. That trade is the single most important choice a printer operator makes.

3. Flow: what goes in must come out

  • Filament is a cylinder 1.75 mm across, so its cross-section is A = πr² = 2.41 mm².
  • It leaves the nozzle as a flat road, layer height tall and about a nozzle-width across.
  • The volume is the same going in and coming out, so the feed rate must be vfilament = (Aroad ÷ Afilament) × vprint.
  • Because the road is so much thinner than the filament, the feed is slow — a few millimetres a second while the nozzle races along.
  • A hotend can only melt so fast. Push past roughly 12 mm³/s and it under-extrudes, leaving gaps.

4. Heat: melting and sticking

  • The nozzle must be hot enough to melt the plastic and to re-melt the top of the layer below, so the two weld together.
  • Too cold and the layers sit on each other without joining — the part splits along the layer lines.
  • Too hot and the plastic runs, strings between features and loses its edges.
  • The bed is heated so the first layer stays stuck. Plastic shrinks as it cools, and a cold bed lets the corners curl up.
  • Each plastic has its own window. PLA melts low and barely shrinks; ABS needs more heat and shrinks much more, which is why it lifts so easily. The Filament buttons set the two temperatures to that window — watch the read-out climb to them before the first layer goes down.

5. Motion: three axes

  • X slides the toolhead left and right along the gantry rail.
  • Y slides the bed forward and back — the nozzle does not move that way at all. Between them, X and Y can reach any point in one flat layer.
  • Z lifts the whole gantry by exactly one layer height when a layer is finished. Watch the Z reading climb in steps, never smoothly.
  • The printer's own screen, on the front of the body, shows the three coordinates it is being driven to. The Axis Read-out button puts the same figures in a larger panel over the scene, for a class that cannot see the little screen.
  • Those readings are the coordinates the machine is being commanded to, in millimetres. Drag the view around to see each axis from a different side, or use the View buttons — Spool is where the scene opens, far enough back to hold the machine, the reel stand and all four feeds at once. Spin turns the whole scene slowly so a class can see every side of the part without anyone touching the mouse.
  • Show mesh strips the shading away and draws every surface as the triangles it is really made of — the machine, the reels and the part being printed. A curve on a computer is never a curve: it is a great many flat faces, and this is where that becomes obvious.
  • Time is compressed about fourfold, or the wait would be half an hour. Fast runs it at forty times, to get to a finished object quickly; Slow drops it back to real time, which is the setting to use when the question is what the nozzle is doing right now rather than what the part will look like.
  • The reels on the stand beside the printer are an AMS — an automatic material system. It holds four spools of the same plastic in four different colours, each fed down its own tube. The Filament buttons choose the plastic, which applies to all four; the colour is whichever reel is feeding. Only that reel turns.
  • The reels are lettered A to D. Each object comes on its own reel by default — the Colour buttons override that, and Auto hands the choice back to the model.
  • Print the Pot to see the unit earn its keep: it changes reel a quarter of the way up, and again at half and three quarters, so one object comes out in all four colours with nobody touching it.
  • Print time is simply path length ÷ speed, added up over every layer.
  • At rest the head sits at the top of its travel with the plate centred beneath it. Press Start and it comes down to the corner where the first layer begins; press Reset and it travels back up. The first layer is printed at half speed so it has time to stick down, as a real machine does.
  • The yellow knob on the front of the toolhead is the extruder wheel. It turns one way while plastic is being laid, and briefly the other way when the nozzle hops between separate parts of a layer — that is the machine retracting, pulling the filament back so the hot nozzle does not dribble across the gap.
  • Going faster costs accuracy: the moving mass has to be accelerated and stopped at every corner, and it overshoots.

6. Try this

  • Halve the layer height from 0.20 to 0.10 mm. What happens to the layer count, and to the time?
  • Watch the flow. Double the print speed and see the feed rate double with it — then keep going until the hotend cannot keep up.
  • Turn the bed off and print in ABS. Why do the corners lift, and why is PLA more forgiving?
  • Run the nozzle cold at 175°C with PETG. The shape still prints — so why would the part be weak?
  • Find the fastest good print. Which single setting buys the most time for the least loss of quality?
  • Watch one line go down. Turn on Slow and follow the nozzle for a few seconds. How much of its travel is laying plastic, and how much is crossing a gap with nothing coming out?
  • Print the dog, then the cube. The cube is shorter, yet it takes three times as long. Where does the time actually go?
  • Follow one axis at a time. Watch only the bed for ten seconds, then only the toolhead. Which axis moves continuously, and which one waits and then jumps?
Physics behind the simulation

N = hobject / hlayer

How many layers the object is sliced into.

Afilament = π (d/2)²

Cross-section of the 1.75 mm filament: 2.41 mm².

Aroad = hlayer × wline

Cross-section of the extruded line, roughly a nozzle-width wide.

Q = Aroad × vprint

Volume flow rate, in mm³ per second — what the hotend has to melt.

vfilament = Q / Afilament

Conservation of volume: the feed rate that keeps the flow supplied.

t = Σ (Llayer / vprint)

Print time: every layer's path length divided by speed, added together.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • Each object is built from a few simple solids, and every layer is treated as: once round the outline, then enough line to fill it to 15%. A real slicer varies the path a great deal more.
  • The nozzle is shown tracing a rectangle around the widest part of each layer. A real toolhead follows the true outline, corners and all.
  • The nozzle is 0.4 mm and the extruded line 0.42 mm wide, which is typical.
  • The bead of plastic leaving the nozzle is drawn a little thicker than life, so that it stays visible on screen; it still gets fatter as you raise the layer height.
  • Speed is treated as constant. A real printer accelerates and slows at every corner, so real prints take longer than the arithmetic suggests.
  • The heaters are shown climbing towards the set value at a fixed rate, which is roughly right for a small machine but is not modelled from the element's power.
  • The Filament buttons are a temperature preset: they move the nozzle and bed to the plastic's working range, and the read-out shows the heaters climbing to it. Nothing about the printed result changes with them — what heat does to a print is described above, not simulated.
