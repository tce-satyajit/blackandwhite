Aircraft Landing Gear — The Physics of a Safe Landing

Momentum, impulse and Hooke’s law in one mechanism

1. Coming down

  • A descending aircraft carries momentum p = m v downward and kinetic energy KE = ½ m v².
  • Landing means getting rid of both.
  • Doubling the speed doubles the momentum but quadruples the energy — they do not scale together.
  • Watch the attitude: the main gear touches first and carries the whole load on its own, nose held up. The nose wheel is flown down a couple of seconds later, once the aeroplane is settled.

2. The gear is a spring and a damper

  • The strut squashes by x and pushes back by Hooke’s law F = k x — twice the squash, twice the push.
  • A damper adds a second force c v that depends on how fast it is squashing. That is what stops the aircraft bouncing like a ball.
  • Sitting still, the strut settles where the spring balances the weight: x = mg / k.

3. Newton’s second law

  • F = m a. While the gear pushes up harder than gravity pulls down, the aircraft decelerates.
  • The stronger that push, the harder the jolt felt by the airframe — shown on screen as a g-load.

4. Impulse — the key idea

  • J = F Δt = Δp.
  • The momentum to be removed is fixed the moment you touch down: it depends only on mass and descent speed.
  • But you choose how long to take over it. Spread the same momentum change over a longer time and the force falls in proportion.
  • That is the whole job of a landing gear — not to absorb momentum faster, but slower.

5. On the runway

  • Friction slows the roll: F = μ N.
  • N is the force the wheels actually press with, so during the impact peak the braking is briefly stronger too.
  • If the strut runs out of travel it hits a hard stop and the force spikes — real structural damage.

6. Try this

  • Double the landing speed. Land at 1.0, then 2.0 m/s. Did the peak force double? Did the energy?
  • Increase the mass from 20 t to 80 t. Does the gear squash further, push harder, or both? Watch the g-load.
  • Stiffen the spring from 0.5 to 5.0 MN/m. Compare peak force and impact time. Is stopping sooner better?
  • Why does a longer impact reduce peak force? Find two settings with the same mass and speed but different impact times — the impulse is identical in both.
