Car Gearbox — How Gears Turn Torque into Speed

Learning outcomes, challenges and the physics behind the model

1. Learning outcomes

  • Explain torque as a turning effort, τ = F × r, and say why it is not the same thing as force.
  • Read a gear ratio as a trade: turning force is multiplied by the ratio, turning speed is divided by it.
  • Follow one chain from cause to effect: engine torque → wheel torque → driving force → acceleration → speed.
  • Predict which gear gives the greatest wheel torque, and which allows the highest speed.
  • Use a = F / m to explain why a heavier car accelerates more slowly with the same engine.
  • Resolve weight on a slope and use F = mg sinθ to explain why hills need a lower gear.
  • Recognise that a gearbox creates no extra energy — it trades speed for force, and back again.

2. Try this

Tick marks appear as you manage each one. Reset clears them.

  • ☐ 1. Which gear gives the greatest wheel torque? Drive gently below 40 km/h and step through all five gears, watching the wheel torque.
  • ☐ 2. Which gear is best for starting the car? Reset, then pull away from a standstill.
  • ☐ 3. Which gear is best for cruising fast? Get past 100 km/h.
  • ☐ 4. Can you climb a steep hill? Set the slope to 10° or more and still accelerate.
  • ☐ 5. Keep accelerating while changing gear. Shift up without the acceleration falling to zero.

3. What the gearbox is doing

  • A small gear driving a large one turns slowly but strongly. That is 1st gear.
  • A large gear driving a small one turns quickly but weakly. That is 5th gear.
  • The gearbox never adds energy. Whatever it multiplies the force by, it divides the speed by.
  • A gear only helps while the engine is turning in its strong range. Held at the red line, 1st gear is actually weaker at the wheel than 2nd — changing up drops the revs back onto the torque peak. That is why the driver shifts, and why the rev counter matters.
Physics behind the simulation

τ = F × r

Torque is a turning effort: a force applied at a distance from the centre. The same force further out turns harder.

Gear Ratio = Input Speed / Output Speed

A ratio of 4:1 means the input shaft turns four times for one turn of the output. Speed is divided by four, and turning force is multiplied by four.

τwheel = τengine × Gear Ratio × efficiency

The gears multiply the engine's turning effort. Some is lost to friction, so an efficiency of about 0.90 is used.

F = τ / r

Wheel torque becomes a push at the road. A smaller wheel turns the same torque into a bigger push.

F = m a

Newton's second law. The net force, after the resistances are taken off, is what accelerates the car.

Froll = Crr m g cosθ

Rolling resistance: the tyres flexing against the road. Here Crr = 0.015.

Fslope = m g sinθ

On a hill, part of the car's weight acts back along the road. At 10° that is about 17% of the car's weight.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The five gearbox ratios feed a fixed final drive of 3.70:1 at the axle, as in a real car. The ratio shown in the chain is the total, gearbox × final drive — without it the ratios given would send the car past 900 km/h.
  • The clutch is either fully engaged or fully out. There is no slipping, and no gap in the drive while shifting.
  • The engine's torque curve is a simple parabola peaking at 3500 rpm. Real curves are lumpier.
  • Air resistance is left out, so the only resistances are rolling and slope. Instead, the car cannot exceed the speed its RPM and gear allow — which is the real reason you must shift up.
  • The wheels are assumed not to spin or skid; all the driving force reaches the road.
