Bench Drill — Speed, Torque and the Belt That Sets Them

One motor, one speed — and five different machines, depending on where the belt sits

1. The belt is the whole point

  • The motor in the head runs at one speed and one speed only — about 1400 rpm, whatever you are doing.
  • Everything else comes from where the belt sits. Lift the guard and you can see two stepped pulleys, one on the motor and one on the spindle, facing each other the opposite way up.
  • A belt does not stretch or slip, so the same length of belt passes over both pulleys every second. That single fact gives you the whole rule: n1 × d1 = n2 × d2.
  • Put the belt on a big motor pulley driving a small spindle pulley and the spindle spins faster than the motor. The other way round and it spins slower. Five pairs, five speeds, from 600 to 3267 rpm — a range of more than five to one, from a motor that never changes.
  • This is the same rule as gears on a bicycle, and the same rule as the gears in the car gearbox: you never get something for nothing. Read on.

2. Cutting speed: what the edge actually feels

  • The spindle speed is not what matters to the drill. What matters is how fast the cutting edge travels through the metal, and that depends on how far out from the centre the edge is.
  • The outer corner of the drill goes round a circle of circumference πD. So the cutting speed is Vc = π × D × n, usually quoted in metres a minute.
  • That is why a wide bit must run slowly. At 1400 rpm a 4 mm bit's edge does 18 m/min, which is gentle; a 20 mm bit's edge does 88 m/min, which will burn.
  • Every material has a speed its edge can survive. Softwood will take about 90 m/min, aluminium about 70, mild steel only about 25. Go much past it and the edge gets hot enough to lose its hardness — and once it is blunt it is blunt.
  • Turn the bit up to 20 mm on mild steel and read what the machine says. The right speed is under 400 rpm and the slowest step is 600, so the machine cannot go slow enough. That is a real limitation of a real bench drill, not a fault in the model.

3. Speed and turning effort: the bargain

  • The motor delivers a fixed power, and power is turning effort times turning speed: P = τ × ω.
  • So if you gear for speed you lose torque, and if you gear for torque you lose speed. Step 1 gives you five times the turning effort of step 5, and one fifth of the rpm. The belt cannot create energy; it can only trade one thing for the other.
  • There is a second limit, and it is not the motor. A belt can only pull so hard before it slips, and the torque it can pass to the spindle is that pull times the spindle pulley's radius. In bottom gear the spindle pulley is the big one, so the belt can carry a lot — but bottom gear is also where the motor is trying to deliver its greatest torque, and the two do not rise at the same rate.
  • Work it through and the machine divides in two: in the low steps the belt gives way first, in the high steps the motor does. Find the step where the changeover happens — the read-out names the limit you are up against, so you can hunt for it.
  • So gearing down does not buy you unlimited turning effort. Somewhere the drive gives up whichever way you go.

4. Feed: how thick a chip you take

  • Feed rate is how fast the bit is pushed down. Divide it by the spindle speed and you get the feed per revolution — the thickness of the shaving each turn takes off.
  • A thicker chip needs more force to shear, so the torque and the thrust both go up with feed. That is why forcing the drill makes it bog down.
  • And here is the trap. When the drill bogs down, changing down a step looks like the obvious cure — more torque. But hold the feed at the same millimetres a minute and halving the rpm doubles the feed per revolution, so the chip gets twice as thick and asks for twice the torque. The gain and the cost are the same factor and they cancel exactly. Against the motor's limit a gear change buys you nothing whatever; only a lighter feed or a smaller bit will do. Try it: stall it in top gear, then walk it down step by step and watch the two readings stay in the same ratio the whole way.
  • Against the belt's limit it is a different story, because that limit does not scale with rpm — it is the pull times the spindle pulley's radius. There, changing step really does move the line, and it is changing up that helps.
  • But feed is also the whole of how long the job takes: t = thickness ÷ feed rate. Feed too gently and the bit rubs instead of cutting, gets hot, and takes all day.
  • Notice that rpm does not appear in the time. Spinning faster does not drill the hole faster; only feeding faster does. Speed decides whether the edge survives, feed decides how long it takes.

5. The workpiece twists back

  • The drill twists the metal. By Newton's third law the metal twists the drill back — and it twists the whole workpiece with exactly the same torque.
  • The only thing stopping the workpiece spinning round is friction from the clamp: Ffriction = μ × Fclamp, acting at the bolt circle.
  • Wind the clamp force down and drill a big hole in steel. The moment the drilling torque beats the friction, the workpiece snatches round. In a workshop that is how people lose fingers, and it is why you clamp the work and never hold it.
  • It is worst just as the drill breaks through the far side, when the last thin web of metal tears rather than cuts, and the bit grabs.

6. Try this

  • Find the right gear. Set a 12 mm bit in mild steel. Which belt step gets the cutting speed nearest 25 m/min? Now switch to wood without moving the belt — what happens to the cutting speed, and is it still right?
  • Prove the bargain. Note the torque on step 1, then move to step 5 without touching anything else. Multiply each torque by its rpm. Why are the two answers nearly the same?
  • Press Drill and the machine switches on: the light on the head goes green, the motor winds up to speed, and only then does the drill go down — what the feed lever does is push an already turning drill into the work. When the hole is through and the quill is back up it switches off again, and you can watch the spindle coast to a stop long after the light has gone out. That is why you never reach for the chuck the moment you flick the switch.
  • Make it stall. Leave the belt in top gear and wind the feed up on steel. Does it stop because the motor runs out of power, or because the belt slips? The read-out will tell you which.
  • Time it. Double the spindle speed and predict the drilling time. Then double the feed rate and predict again. Only one of them changes the answer — which, and why?
  • Let go of the work. Take the clamp force to zero on a 16 mm hole in steel. At what torque does it let go, and where in the hole does it happen?
Physics behind the simulation

nspindle = nmotor × dmotor ÷ dspindle

The belt rule. The same belt speed over both pulleys.

Vc = π × D × n ÷ 1000

Cutting speed at the outer edge, in metres a minute for D in mm.

frev = feed rate ÷ n

How far the drill advances each turn — the chip thickness.

τ = kc × D² × frev ÷ 8

Torque the cut demands. kc is the material's specific cutting force.

Fthrust = kc × D × frev ÷ 2

The push down the quill has to supply.

P = τ × ω,   ω = 2πn ÷ 60

Power the cut asks of the motor.

τbelt max = Fbelt × rspindle pulley

What the belt can pass on before it slips — less on a small pulley.

τhold = μ × Fclamp × rclamp

What the clamp's friction can resist before the work spins.

t = thickness ÷ feed rate

Drilling time. Note what is not in it.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The cutting force model is the standard textbook approximation for a two-flute twist drill. Real cutting forces depend on the point angle, the web thickness, how sharp the drill is and whether it is cooled, none of which are modelled.
  • The motor is treated as delivering constant power once up to speed, and as simply refusing to go beyond it. A real induction motor slows down under load and its torque curve is nothing like flat.
  • Belt slip is modelled as a hard limit. A real belt slips progressively, squeals, gets hot and wears.
  • The bit is assumed to stay sharp. Running it too fast is reported as a warning, but the edge never actually blunts and the readings never change because of it.
  • Break-through grab is modelled as a rise in torque over the last part of the hole. The real effect depends on how the drill point is ground.
  • No coolant, no pecking, no chip clearance problems in deep holes — all of which matter a great deal in a real workshop.
