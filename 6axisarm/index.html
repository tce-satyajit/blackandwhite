6-Axis Robot Arm — How a Machine Reaches, Grips and Places

Six rotations, one point in space — and the turning effort that limits them all

1. Six axes, and why six

  • Every joint on this robot is a revolute joint: it does nothing but rotate about one line. There is not a single sliding part in the arm.
  • To put an object at a chosen place you need three numbers — x, y, z. To also choose which way it faces you need three more. Three plus three is six, and that is exactly how many joints this machine has.
  • J1 swings the whole arm round. J2 and J3 are the shoulder and elbow, and between them they set how far out and how high the wrist is. J4, J5 and J6 are the wrist, and they aim the tool.
  • Watch J6 earn its keep. The parts are left on the table at all angles, and a rectangular carton will not travel well on a belt lying across it. So the arm carries it over still lying the way it was picked up, and turns it as it sets it down — short side across the belt, long side running with the travel. Everything else about the move would be the same on a five-axis machine; that one turn is what the sixth axis is for.
  • A quarter turn, note, not a half. Turning a rectangle through 180° maps it exactly onto itself — same footprint, same fit. Only an odd quarter turn actually swaps which side crosses the belt, and a round drum does not care either way.
  • Turn on Axes and each joint's line of rotation is drawn in its own colour. Watch one at a time: everything past that joint moves, everything before it stays still.
  • Press Show Axes and the robot runs each of the six through its own travel in turn, naming it as it goes. It is the quickest way to see what every joint is actually for.
  • Watch for what the pick-and-place programme does not use. With the tool held pointing straight down, J4 and J6 turn about the same line — so the programme leaves J4 at zero and lets J6 do the work. Two axes doing one job is called a singularity, and it is why the demonstration is the only place you will see J4 move. Turning J4 earns its keep the moment the tool has to be tilted.
  • A machine with fewer axes can still reach the point, but it cannot choose the angle it arrives at. That is why a five-axis arm cannot always do a six-axis job.

2. Working out the angles: the two-link triangle

  • Given the angles, finding where the tool ends up is easy — you follow the arm along. That is forward kinematics.
  • The useful problem is the other way round: you know where the tool must go, and you need the angles. That is inverse kinematics, and it is what this simulation solves sixty times a second.
  • The trick is that the upper arm, the forearm and the straight line from shoulder to wrist make a triangle whose three sides are known. The cosine rule gives you the angles: cos C = (a² + b² − c²) ÷ 2ab.
  • That triangle can usually be made in two ways — elbow up or elbow down. A real robot has to be told which, and both put the tool in exactly the same place.
  • If the point is further away than the two links laid end to end, there is no triangle at all, and no answer. The arm simply cannot get there.

3. Reach, and the working envelope

  • Turn on Envelope to see the shell the tool point can reach: the outer surface is the arm fully straight, the inner hole is the arm folded up against itself.
  • Reach and payload pull against each other. Every millimetre further out costs turning effort at the shoulder, so a long-reach robot always carries less.
  • Drag a part to the very edge of the envelope and the arm straightens out. Straight out is where it is weakest and least accurate — a small error in the shoulder angle becomes a large error at the tool.

4. Turning effort: why payload falls off with reach

  • A mass held out on an arm is a moment about the shoulder: τ = m g r, where r is the horizontal distance out from the shoulder.
  • The weight itself never changes. What changes is the lever arm. Hold the same box twice as far out and the shoulder motor works twice as hard.
  • Torque along the top of the window shows the live figure, and turns red when it passes what the joint is rated for. Wind the Payload up and watch it rise and fall through the cycle — it peaks when the arm is stretched out, not when the part is highest.
  • This is the same reason you can hold a full bag against your chest all day but not at arm's length.

5. Getting there: speed, acceleration and time

  • The tool does not jump to full speed. It speeds up at the acceleration you set, holds the tool speed if it gets there, then slows down again in time to stop.
  • Draw that as a speed–time graph and it is a trapezium. The area under it is the distance travelled.
  • On a short move there is no time to reach full speed at all, so the trapezium collapses to a triangle: it accelerates to the middle and brakes from there. Raising the speed limit then changes nothing, and only more acceleration helps.
  • The threshold is d = v² ÷ a. Below that distance the move is triangular, above it trapezoidal. Try 2000 mm/s with a gentle acceleration and watch the short descents ignore you completely.

6. Straight lines and joint moves

  • Straight holds the tool point on a straight line, and the six joints are made to do whatever that needs. The path is predictable, which matters when there is something in the way.
  • Notice that the arm does not always take the same route. Going from the table to the belt it swings out round the front of the cell, because a straight line between them would drag the part through the robot's own shoulder. Going from the ready pose to the table it just goes, because nothing is in the way. Choosing which via points a move needs, and leaving out the ones it does not, is most of what writing a robot programme actually is.
  • Joint simply runs every joint from its start angle to its finish angle together. It is quicker and easier on the motors — but the tool swings out through a curve on the way, and that curve is where collisions happen.
  • Turn on the Trail and switch between the two. The two paths start and finish at the same points and are nothing alike in between.

7. The tool: two ways to hold the same box

  • The robot is only half the machine. What it can actually do depends on what is bolted to the end of it, and the two tools here hold a part by completely different physics.
  • Vacuum. Nothing sucks a part upwards. The pump takes air out from under the cups, and the atmosphere pushes the part on from every other side. The force is the pressure difference times the sealed area, F = Δp × A: four cups of 26 mm radius is about 85 cm², and at 55 kPa that is roughly 470 N — near enough 48 kg hanging still.
  • Jaws. Two faces squeezed onto the sides. Squeezing alone lifts nothing: what stops the part sliding out of the grip is friction, F = 2 μN for the two faces. At 340 N of clamp and a rubber-on-card μ of 0.55, that is about 370 N. Squeeze harder or use grippier pads and it rises; use polished steel pads and the part slides straight out however hard you clamp.
  • They are parallel jaws, not scissor jaws. Parallel faces stay flat against a box and tangent to a drum at any opening; a scissor pair meets at an angle, so it pinches a box at two corners and tends to roll a round part out of the grip as it closes.
  • Neither tool only carries the weight. Every time the arm speeds up or slows down the part must be accelerated with it, and that comes through the tool too: F = m(g + a).
  • So acceleration eats your payload. Hold along the top shows what the part is asking of the tool against what the tool can give; wind the acceleration up with a heavy part and it turns red, and the robot's own screen reads LOSING GRIP — not because the part got heavier, but because it is being thrown about harder. This is why a cell that runs beautifully at half speed starts dropping boxes when someone speeds it up.
  • And the tool has to be able to get at the part in the first place. Jaws reach in from the sides, so they cannot pick a thin panel lying flat — there is nowhere to put them that is not the table, or the panel underneath. With the jaws fitted the Panel is simply not offered, because it is not a thing the machine can do. That is the job suction was invented for.

8. The conveyor, and what a cell is worth

  • A robot on its own does nothing useful. It is worth something when it is part of a cell: something to pick from, something to place onto, and a cycle that repeats.
  • The parts come stacked, and are taken a layer at a time off the top down — which is what depalletising means. Watch the Z reading at each pick: it comes down by exactly one part height every time a layer is cleared. Cartons stack two high and panels four; a drum stood on its end does not stack at all, which is a fact about the part and nothing to do with the robot.
  • Cycle time is how long one part takes, start to finish. Rate is simply 60 ÷ cycle time parts a minute.
  • The gap the parts land at on the belt is belt speed × cycle time. Speed the belt up and they spread out; speed the robot up and they bunch together. Set both and you have designed a production line.
  • Notice that the belt never stops for the robot. The part is released onto something already moving, and it takes the belt's speed the instant it is let go.

9. Try this

  • Find the triangle. Turn on Axes and Slow, and watch only J2 and J3 through one descent. Can you see the two links closing on the wrist?
  • Break the arm's back. Put the payload at 30 kg and watch the torque figure through a cycle. At which moment is the shoulder working hardest, and why is it not when the part is highest?
  • Make speed useless. Set acceleration to its lowest and tool speed to its highest. Which moves get faster, and which do not care at all?
  • Straight against Joint. Turn on the Trail and switch the path type. Which is quicker, and what has been given up to get it?
  • Design a line. Choose a belt speed that puts exactly one part width of gap between parts. What happens to that gap when you speed the robot up?
  • Count the axes. Which joints would you have to remove before the robot could no longer set the carton down flat?
  • Drop a box without touching the payload. Leave the mass alone and raise only the acceleration until the tool lets go. Why does going faster make a part heavier to hold?
  • Change the tool, not the robot. Run the same carton on the vacuum head and on the jaws. Which holds more, and why is the answer nothing to do with the arm?
  • Find the job each tool cannot do. Put the jaws on the panel. What exactly stops them, and would a bigger robot help at all?
Physics behind the simulation

J1 = atan2(−z, x)

The base swings until the whole arm lies in the vertical plane through the target.

D² = Δr² + Δy²

Straight-line distance from the shoulder to the wrist, once the plane is chosen.

cos J3 = (D² − L2² − L3²) ÷ 2 L2 L3

The cosine rule on the shoulder–elbow–wrist triangle gives the elbow angle.

J2 = atan2(Δy, Δr) + acos((D² + L2² − L3²) ÷ 2 D L2)

Point the upper arm at the wrist, then swing it up by the triangle's other angle.

J5 = −90° − J2 − J3

Whatever the shoulder and elbow did, the wrist takes it back out so the tool still points straight down.

τ = m g r

Turning effort at the shoulder: the weight of the part times how far out it is held.

t = d/v + v/a   (if d > v²/a)   else   t = 2√(d/a)

How long a move takes, with and without room to reach full speed.

Fvacuum = Δp × A     Fjaws = 2 μN     Fneeded = m(g + a)

What each tool can hold, against what this part and this acceleration ask of it.

gap = vbelt × tcycle

How far apart the parts end up on the conveyor.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The wrist is treated as a perfect spherical wrist, with J4, J5 and J6 crossing at one point. Real wrists are close to this but not exact.
  • The elbow is always solved elbow-up. The elbow-down solution reaches the same points and is simply not used here.
  • The tool is held pointing straight down throughout, which is what a pick-and-place job needs. A real programme would tilt it.
  • The torque figure counts the payload only. The arm's own weight is a large part of a real robot's load and is not included.
  • Acceleration is applied along the path, not joint by joint. A real controller works to each joint's own limit, whichever binds first.
  • The joints have no backlash, no flex and no settling time, so the tool arrives exactly where it was sent. Accuracy and repeatability are the whole difficulty in a real machine.
  • The vacuum is treated as perfect: the cups seal completely, and the pump holds its full 55 kPa the whole time. A leaking cup, a dusty carton or a curved surface all cost a great deal of that in practice.
  • The jaws are treated as squeezing with a fixed force at a fixed coefficient of friction, and as never crushing the part. Choosing a clamp force that holds a carton without staving it in is most of the work of designing a real gripper.
  • The warning says the tool would lose the part; the part is not actually dropped. Working out where it would land is a projectile problem, and a different lesson.
