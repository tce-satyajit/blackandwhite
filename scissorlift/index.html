Scissor Lift — Hydraulics and the Mechanical Advantage That Will Not Sit Still

Grades 8–10 · Pressure, moments, mechanical advantage, work and power, and stability

1. Pressure, and why oil can lift a car

  • Pressure is force spread over area: p = F / A, measured in pascals. One bar is 100 000 Pa, near enough the pressure of the atmosphere.
  • A liquid cannot be squashed, and Pascal’s principle says the pressure you put into it is felt everywhere in it at once. So a small pump piston pushing hard on a little area raises the pressure of the whole system — and that same pressure then pushes on the big area of the ram.
  • Rearrange it: F = p × A. Double the ram’s diameter and you quadruple its area, because area goes as the square of the radius. Try the bore slider and watch the pressure needed collapse.
  • There is no free lunch in it. The big ram moves proportionally less far than the little pump piston, so the work going in and the work coming out are the same. Hydraulics is a lever made of oil.

2. The scissor: a mechanical advantage that keeps changing

  • Here is the thing that makes a scissor lift worth studying. Its mechanical advantage is not a number you can look up — it changes all the way up the lift, and it changes enormously.
  • Watch the ram force as you raise it from the bottom. Near the floor the arms are almost flat, and holding the deck up takes something like twenty times the weight on it. By the top it is nearer three. Same lift, same load, same rams.
  • Why? Because at a shallow angle the ram is pushing almost along the arm rather than across it, and almost none of its push turns into a turning effect about the pivot. The useful part of a force is the part at right angles to the arm.
  • Now watch the speed at the same time. It bolts away from the floor and then crawls near the top. That is not a coincidence, and it is not the pump changing: it is the same trade, read the other way round.
  • Work it out with work. The ram does F × s and the load gains W × h, and those must be equal, so F/W = h/s. If the force ratio is twenty, then one millimetre of ram must be buying twenty millimetres of height. High force ratio and high speed ratio are the same fact.

3. Moments: why it is hardest at the bottom

  • The moment, or turning effect, of a force about a pivot is τ = F × d, where d is the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of the force.
  • That word perpendicular is the whole story. With the arms nearly flat the ram’s line of push passes very close to the pivot, so d is tiny and even a huge force produces almost no turning.
  • It is the same reason you push a door at the handle and not at the hinge, and the same reason a spanner works better when it is long.
  • This is why real scissor lifts stall on the floor and not in the air, and why they are built with the rams as steep as the frame will allow.

4. Work, energy and power

  • Lifting a load stores gravitational potential energy: Ep = mgh. Put 1000 kg up 1.4 m and you have banked about 13.7 kJ — and you get it back on the way down, which is why lowering needs no pump at all, only a valve let open.
  • Power is the rate of doing work, P = W/t, in watts. For a hydraulic system it comes out as P = p × Q — pressure times flow. High pressure and a trickle, or low pressure and a torrent, can be the same power.
  • The pump’s flow is the only thing that sets ram speed: the oil has to go somewhere, and the ram is the only place it can go. Change the load and the pressure changes; the speed does not.
  • Nothing is free: bearings rub and oil is forced through pipes, so the motor always draws more than the load receives. That ratio is the efficiency.

5. Standing up: stability and tipping

  • A thing stays put while its centre of gravity is over its base. Push the load out to the edge of the deck and the weight’s line of action walks towards the wheels.
  • Compare two moments about the tipping wheel: the load trying to turn it over, and the machine’s own weight holding it down. When the first beats the second, it goes.
  • Height makes it worse, and the model shows why: the higher the deck, the further a small push at the top can shift things, and a wheeled base is a poor anchor. This is why real lifts are chocked, and why load charts get stricter with height.
  • The wheels under this one are for moving it between jobs, not for working on. Put the load right out to 600 mm and watch what the read-out says.

6. Try this

  • Find the hard part. Load it to 1500 kg and raise it from flat. Watch the pressure gauge as it climbs. Where is the pressure worst — and is that where you expected?
  • Stall it, then cheat. Turn the relief valve down to 120 bar and try to lift 2000 kg off the floor. It will not go. Now raise the lift halfway by hand (lower the load, lift, put the load back) and try again from there. Why does the same lift now manage the same weight?
  • Prove the bargain. Note the ram force and the lift speed near the bottom, then again near the top. Multiply each force by its speed. Why do the two answers come out so close?
  • Area beats effort. Leave everything alone and take the bore from 50 mm to 130 mm. Predict what happens to the pressure before you look — is it halved, or something else? Then explain what happened to the speed.
  • Power, not force. Double the pump flow and watch the motor power. Then double the load instead. Which one costs the motor more, and why?
  • Tip it. Put 2000 kg at 600 mm off centre and raise it. Read the tipping margin. What could you change — other than the load — to make it safe?
Physics behind the simulation

p = F / A,   A = πd²/4

Pressure and ram area. Bore is a diameter, so the area goes as its square.

h = 2L sinθ

Deck height for a two-stage scissor of arm length L at half-angle θ.

s = √(p² + q² − 2pq cosθ)

Ram length, straight from the cosine rule on the mounting triangle.

F × δs = W × δh  →  F/W = (dh/dθ) / (ds/dθ)

Virtual work. The whole mechanical advantage falls out of this one line, with no need to resolve a single force.

F/W = 2L cosθ · s / (p q sinθ)

What that comes to here. As θ → 0 it goes to infinity, which is the flat-on-the-floor problem.

vram = Q / (n A),   vdeck = vram × (F/W)

Flow sets ram speed; the linkage ratio turns it into deck speed.

P = p × Q,   Pmotor = P / η

Hydraulic power, and what the motor has to draw to supply it.

τtip = m g x,   τhold = M g b

Overturning against restoring, taken about the wheel on the tipping side.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The arms are treated as weightless in the force balance, and only the deck and its load are counted. Real scissor arms are heavy, and near the bottom their own weight matters.
  • The linkage is frictionless. In a real machine the pin joints and the roller take a real bite out of the ram’s effort, worst of all where the loads are highest.
  • The pump is treated as delivering a steady flow whatever the pressure, up to the relief setting, where it dumps everything. A real pump slips more as pressure rises.
  • Oil is taken as incompressible and the hoses as rigid. Neither is quite true, and it is why a real lift sinks a little when you first load it.
  • Lowering is a controlled leak-down at a fixed rate rather than a proper flow calculation through the valve.
  • The tipping check is a static one about the wheel line. It says nothing about a lift being pushed, or standing on a slope, or being shock-loaded - all of which are what actually turns them over.
