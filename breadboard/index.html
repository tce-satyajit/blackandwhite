Breadboard — The Hidden Rows and What a Circuit Needs

A board with 830 holes and only about 130 actual connections

1. The board is not a grid of holes

  • Every hole has a metal clip behind it, and the clips are joined in strips of five. Push two wires into the same row of five and they are connected — not because they touch, but because one strip of springy metal is gripping both.
  • Turn on Links and the plastic goes see-through. What looks like 830 separate holes is really about 130 strips, and every one of them is a decision somebody made when the board was moulded.
  • The strips run across the board, five holes at a time. They do not run along it. Two parts in the same column are connected; two parts in the same long row are not.
  • Down the middle is a channel, and the strips stop dead at it. The left five and the right five of the same line are two separate connections. That gap is not decoration — it is there so a chip can straddle it with its legs in different strips.
  • Along the edges are the power rails, marked red and blue. These are the exception: they run the whole length of the board, so power is available anywhere along it. That is why the battery goes there and nowhere else.
  • Nearly every circuit that does not work fails for this one reason: two legs that were meant to be joined are in different strips, or two that were meant to be separate are sharing one.

2. A circuit is a loop, or it is nothing

  • Current does not get used up and it does not flow out to a component. It goes round: out of one terminal of the battery, through everything, and back into the other. Break the loop anywhere and it stops everywhere, instantly.
  • That is all a switch is. It is a deliberate gap you can close. There is nothing else in it.
  • Open the switch and watch the current reading go to zero — not the LED first and the resistor after. The whole loop stops at once.
  • In a series loop the current is the same at every point. There is nowhere else for it to go. Put the meter on Current and move the circuit around: the number is the number, wherever in the loop you break in.

3. Ohm's law, and what the resistor is for

  • V = I × R. Rearranged the way you will actually use it: I = V ÷ R. More push, more current; more resistance, less current.
  • Set the circuit to LED, put the meter Across the resistor, and turn the Knob. Watch the volts across it and the current in the loop move together, always in the same ratio — that ratio is the resistance.
  • The resistor's colour bands in the model are the real code for whatever value you have set. Change the value and watch the bands change: brown-black-brown is 100 Ω, red-red-brown is 220, yellow-violet-brown is 470.
  • A resistor does not "protect" by magic. It takes some of the voltage for itself, and what it takes it turns into heat. Power is P = V × I, and every milliwatt of it leaves as warmth.

4. Why an LED must never go straight across a battery

  • An LED is not a resistor. Below about 1.8 V it passes almost nothing; above it, the current climbs almost vertically. It has no interest in limiting itself.
  • So the current is set by everything else in the loop: I = (Vsupply − Vf) ÷ R. On 6 V through 220 Ω that is about 19 mA, which is exactly what a 5 mm LED wants.
  • Now wind the resistor to 0 and close the switch. There is nothing to set the current but the battery's own internal resistance, the reading goes to hundreds of milliamps, and the LED burns out — in the model as in life, in well under a second.
  • It is worth doing once. The number on screen is the point: 20 mA is normal, 60 mA is fatal, and there is nothing in between to warn you.
  • An LED is also a one-way part. Press Turn LED Round and it simply stops — no light, no current, no damage. The long leg goes to the positive rail; that is the only rule to remember.

5. Series and parallel: the difference that matters

  • Put 2 LEDs on the board and switch between the two wirings with everything else left alone.
  • Series: one loop through both. The same current goes through each, and they share the voltage — about 1.8 V each. On a 3 V battery there is nothing left over and both stay dark. That is not a fault; it is arithmetic.
  • Parallel: two loops side by side. Both LEDs now have the same voltage across them — about 1.8 V each — so both light even on a small battery, which series could not manage.
  • But look at the current. The two LEDs are sharing one resistor, and that resistor is what decides the total. So the current splits: about 9 mA each instead of 18, and both are dimmer than a single LED would have been. Adding a second lamp did not double the current, because nothing let it.
  • That is exactly why each LED in a real circuit gets its own resistor. Share one between them and they share the current too — and if the two LEDs are not identical, the greedier one takes more than its half and the other stays dim.
  • The rule underneath: series adds resistance (R = R1 + R2), parallel reduces it (1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2). Two paths are easier than one.
  • This is why the lights in a house are in parallel. In series, switching one off would kill them all, and each would only get a share of the mains.

6. The motor: a load that pushes back

  • A spinning motor generates a voltage of its own, against the supply — the back-EMF. That is what stops it drawing enormous current once it is up to speed.
  • Hold it still and there is no back-EMF at all, so the current is only limited by the winding's own small resistance. A stalled motor draws far more current than a running one, and that is how motors burn out.
  • Try feeding the motor through 470 Ω. It will not even start — the resistor drops nearly all the voltage before the motor gets any. A motor needs current, not just volts, which is exactly why you cannot run one from the same resistor you would use for an LED.
  • Wind the Knob down slowly with the motor running and find the speed where it gives up. It does not fade away gently; it stops.

7. Measuring: across, or through

  • Voltage is a difference between two points, so a voltmeter goes across a component, touching both sides, with the circuit left alone.
  • Current is a flow, so an ammeter has to be in the loop — you break the circuit and let the current run through the meter. Watch the probes move when you switch the meter to Current: they go into the loop, not across it.
  • Add up the voltages across the resistor and the LED and you get the supply voltage back. Every volt the battery provides is used up somewhere by the time you get back to it. That is Kirchhoff's voltage law, and you can read it straight off the meter.
  • The supply reading is the terminal voltage, not the number printed on the battery. Under a heavy load it sags, because the battery has resistance of its own inside it.

8. Try this

  • Find the joined holes. With Links on and the View set to Top, count how many separate strips there are in one column. Now work out why a part with both legs in the same strip does nothing at all.
  • Predict, then check. 6 V, 220 Ω, one LED. Work out the current on paper first: subtract the 1.8 V the LED takes, divide what is left by 220. Then close the switch and see how close you were.
  • Kirchhoff by hand. Read the volts across the resistor, then across the LED. Add them. Compare with the supply reading. Try to make them not add up.
  • Two LEDs on a small battery. Set the battery to 3 V and put two LEDs in series. Explain the darkness using nothing but the 1.8 V figure. Now switch to parallel without touching anything else.
  • Kill one. Wind the resistor to zero on a 9 V battery and watch the current. Then do the arithmetic: at that current, how many times the rated 20 mA is the LED being asked to pass?
  • Make the motor refuse. Start with the knob wide open and the motor running, then wind it up until it stops. Read the current at the moment it dies, and again just after.
The numbers, and what is left out

I = (V − Vf) ÷ (R + rinternal)

The current in an LED loop. Vf is taken as 1.8 V for a red LED.

Vterminal = EMF − I × rinternal

Why the supply reading sags the moment you ask for current.

V = I Rarmature + k ω

The motor. The second term is the back-EMF, and it is zero when stalled.

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The LED is modelled as a fixed forward voltage plus a small series resistance. A real diode follows an exponential curve, and its forward voltage shifts with both current and temperature.
  • Burn-out is modelled as heating over time above a threshold current. A real LED dies by degrees — it dims and shifts colour long before it fails outright — and a brief overload it would survive is treated here as cumulative.
  • The battery is an ideal EMF behind a fixed internal resistance. A real cell's internal resistance rises as it discharges, and nothing here ever goes flat.
  • The motor is a steady-state model with viscous friction and a fixed load. Starting current, brush arcing, cogging and the inductive kick when the switch opens are all left out — the last of these being exactly why a real motor circuit needs a flyback diode.
  • The meter is perfect: infinite resistance across a component, none at all in the loop. Real meters are neither, which is why a cheap ammeter changes the very current it is measuring.
  • Wires and the board's own strips are treated as having no resistance. On a real breadboard the contacts are worth a fraction of an ohm each, and a tired board is worth a great deal more.
  • The board is drawn as a half-size 400-point board with the strips laid out as they really are, but the components are placed for clarity rather than to a real layout you could copy hole for hole.
