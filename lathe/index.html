What this machine teaches

1. The one thing a lathe teaches that a drill cannot

  • On a drill the tool spins and the work is still. On a lathe it is the other way round: the work spins and the tool is still. What is being cut is the outside of a turning bar.
  • That single swap changes everything, because the cutting speed depends on the diameter where the tool is — and on a lathe that diameter is the thing you are busy changing.
  • Vc = π × D × n. On the drill, D was fixed by whichever bit was in the chuck. Here, every pass makes D smaller, so the cutting speed falls away under you even though the spindle speed has not moved.
  • Turn a 60 mm bar down to 20 mm at one speed and watch the reading: the edge ends up going a third as fast as it started. To keep cutting properly you have to change gear part way through the job. That is not a fault; that is turning.

2. Facing: where the cutting speed goes to nothing

  • Facing cuts the end of the bar square, with the tool travelling inwards from the rim to the centre.
  • As it goes in, D falls to zero — so Vc falls to zero with it. At the very centre the metal is not moving past the tool at all.
  • Watch the last millimetre. The tool stops cutting and starts rubbing, and that little pip left in the middle of a faced end is something you can find on real work.
  • It is the cleanest demonstration there is that v = ωr: same rotation everywhere, no speed at the axis.

3. Feed per revolution — and why time behaves differently here

  • On the drill the feed was in millimetres a minute, and the time was thickness ÷ feed. Spinning faster did not drill the hole any faster.
  • On a lathe the feed is in millimetres per revolution — the tool advances a set amount each time the work goes round. So t = length ÷ (f × n).
  • Now n is in the time. Double the spindle speed and the job takes half as long. That is the opposite of the drill, and it is worth stopping on: the same-looking quantity behaves differently because of how the feed is defined.
  • But doubling n also doubles Vc. Speed helps you two ways and hurts you one, which is the whole trade of machining.

4. Threading: the one time the feed is not yours

  • A screw thread is a helix, and a helix only works if the tool advances exactly one pitch for every turn of the work. Not roughly — exactly, or the second pass does not land in the first pass’s groove.
  • So on a lathe the leadscrew is geared to the spindle for threading, and the feed knob stops doing anything at all. Watch the panel: it changes from Feed to Pitch (geared) and greys out. That is the machine telling you the choice has been taken away.
  • The pitch comes from the diameter — M20 takes 2.5 mm, M30 takes 3.5 — and the vee is cut to about 0.61 × pitch deep, in several passes, because no tool will take it in one.
  • A note on what you are watching: at 800 rpm a chuck turns 80° between one frame and the next, which the eye cannot follow — it reads as a strobe, not a spin. So the spindle is drawn slower than it is really going, with fast speeds squashed more than slow ones. Faster always looks faster, but the picture is not a stopwatch. Every number on the panel, the feed, the forces and the pitch of the sound all run on the true speed. Put Chuck Spin on True to see the real rate, and the strobe with it — a wheel that appears to stand still or run backwards is the same effect that makes wagon wheels do it in films.
  • Behind the acrylic guard at the far end is the answer to “what actually turns the chuck?” — a motor, a small pulley, three vee belts, and a big pulley that is the spindle. Three belts, because one of that size would slip long before it carried 550 W. The big one is about twice the small one, so the chuck turns at about half motor speed and with about twice the turning effort. That is the same trade the bench drill makes on its cone pulleys: n1d1 = n2d2, and you never get speed and torque both.
  • Threading is done slowly. At the end of every pass the tool has to be pulled out of the groove and the carriage wound back, and at turning speeds there is no time to do it. Try it in top gear and the machine will say so.

5. Drilling and boring: cutting on the inside

  • A hole down the middle starts with a drill held in the tailstock and fed straight along the axis. The work spins, the drill does not — the exact opposite of a drilling machine, and the hole comes out the same.
  • Here the cutting speed goes back to being a fixed thing, because the drill’s diameter is fixed. It is the one operation on a lathe that behaves like the bench drill.
  • To make the hole bigger, or straighter, or to a size no drill exists for, you follow it with a boring bar — a tool that reaches down inside and cuts outwards. Each pass takes the depth of cut off the radius, so the hole grows by twice that.
  • And now the cutting speed rises as you go, because the diameter is growing. That is the turning lesson running backwards.

6. The chip, the force and the power

  • The tool is peeling a ribbon whose cross-section is feed × depth of cut. That area is what sets how hard the cut is.
  • Fc = kc × f × ap — kc is how hard the material is to shear, so steel asks about three times what aluminium does for the same chip.
  • That force acts at the radius of the work, so the spindle sees τ = Fc × D ÷ 2. A fat bar is harder on the gearbox than a thin one for the very same cut.
  • And the power it costs is P = Fc × Vc. Take too big a bite and the motor cannot supply it: the reading goes red and the spindle drags to a stop.
  • Depth of cut comes off the radius. Set 1 mm and the diameter drops by 2 mm. Getting that wrong is how a part ends up half the size it should be.

Things to try

  • Cut a thread. Pick Thread and try to change the feed — you cannot, and the panel tells you why. Take pass after pass and watch the vee deepen to 0.61 of the pitch. Then count the turns of the helix along the bar and check it against the pitch.
  • Drill, then bore. Press Cut with Drill & Bore selected: the tailstock brings a drill up and puts a hole down the middle. Press it again and a boring bar takes over. Watch the cutting-speed reading — it now goes up with every pass. Why?
  • Watch the tailstock. Press Cut with Turn selected and the tailstock winds up to the end of the bar and takes it on the centre before a chip is cut — long work bends away from the tool otherwise. Now switch to Face or Part Off: it winds straight back out. Why can it not stay where it was?
  • Watch the speed fall. Put a 70 mm bar in mild steel, pick the speed that gives about 30 m/min, and take pass after pass. How many passes before the cutting speed has halved? What do you have to do about it?
  • Face to the centre. Switch to Face and watch the cutting-speed reading as the tool goes in. Where does it reach zero, and what is the tool doing at that moment?
  • Prove the time rule. Time one pass. Double the spindle speed and predict the new time before you run it. Then go back to the drill simulation and try the same prediction there — it does not work, and the reason is in the units of the feed.
  • Find the motor’s limit. On steel, wind the depth of cut up a quarter of a millimetre at a time until it stalls. Now do it on aluminium. Why is the answer different, and by roughly what factor?
  • Same cut, different bar. Take an identical cut on a 70 mm bar and a 25 mm bar. The chip is the same size and the force is the same — so why does the fat bar load the spindle so much more?
Physics behind the simulation

Vc = π × D × n ÷ 1000

Cutting speed in metres a minute, for D in mm at the tool and n in rev/min. D changes as you cut.

A = f × ap

Cross-section of the chip: feed per revolution times depth of cut.

Fc = kc × A

Cutting force, from the material’s specific cutting force.

τ = Fc × D ÷ 2

Torque at the spindle. The same force at a bigger radius is a bigger moment.

P = Fc × Vc ÷ 60

Cutting power in watts, for F in newtons and Vc in m/min.

t = L ÷ (f × n)

Time for one pass, in minutes. The spindle speed is in this one — unlike drilling.

Dnew = D − 2ap

Depth of cut is taken off the radius, so the diameter falls twice as fast.

A teaching model, not a machining handbook: cutting forces use a single specific-force figure per material and ignore tool geometry, nose radius, rake, built-up edge and tool wear.
