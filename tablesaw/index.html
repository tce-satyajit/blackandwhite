What this machine teaches

1. The opposite case to the lathe

  • On the centre lathe, the diameter under the tool is the thing you are changing, so the cutting speed moves while you work. Nothing you do can hold it still.
  • A table saw is the other case entirely. One blade, one diameter, one speed: v = πDn comes out at about 55 m/s at the rim and stays there. There is no gearbox to change it and no reason to want one.
  • So if the rim speed is fixed, what is left to get wrong? The answer is how the feed is shared out between the teeth.

2. Chip load: the number that decides everything

  • fz = vf ÷ (n × Z) — feed rate, divided by how many teeth come round in that time.
  • Under about 0.012 mm a tooth the edge cannot get under the fibres. It stops cutting and starts rubbing, and rubbing timber at fifty metres a second sets fire to it. That is what a burn mark is: not a blunt blade, a slow one.
  • Over about 0.055 mm the tooth takes more than its gullet can carry out. The chip packs, the edge stops slicing and starts levering, and the top face splinters.
  • The band that works is narrow, and which part of the feed range lands inside it depends entirely on how many teeth you fitted. No single feed rate is right for all three blades.

3. Why an 80-tooth blade is not simply better

  • More teeth means each takes less, so the cut is cleaner. That much is obvious and it is why a crosscut blade has eighty of them.
  • But each of those teeth still has to be pushed through timber, and there are more of them in the cut at once. Put the 80T blade in oak, wind the blade high and feed briskly: the power reading climbs and the arbor speed sags.
  • Worse, at any feed you can manage by hand the 80T blade drops below the rubbing threshold. It is the blade most likely to burn, for exactly the reason it is the blade that cuts cleanest.
  • That is the whole trade, and it is why a workshop owns more than one blade.

4. Teeth in the cut, and what the height wheel really does

  • Only the teeth actually inside the timber are cutting. That is a short arc near the front of the blade, and how long it is depends on how far the blade stands above the table and how thick the board is.
  • Wind the blade higher and the arc gets longer, so more teeth are in the cut, so the force and the power both rise — even though the chip load per tooth has not changed at all.
  • This is why the old advice is to set the blade just proud of the timber. It is not only about how much blade is exposed; it is about asking the motor for less.

5. What the tilt wheel costs you

  • The whole trunnion swings: arbor, motor, belt and blade together. Watch the drive while you tilt it — the belt goes over with everything else, which is why it never has to change length.
  • The blade pivots about a point on the table line, so the highest tooth is carried round an arc and what is left standing above the table is h cos β. The projection reading under the height slider falls as you tilt.
  • Every saw quotes two depths for this reason. Wind it to 98 mm and tilt to 45°: 98 cos 45 = 69 mm, which is what a real 12 inch saw is sold as cutting. It is not a limitation of the motor. It is geometry.

6. The riving knife, and why it is not optional

  • The teeth at the back of the blade are travelling upwards and toward you. If the two halves of the board close on them, the board is picked up and thrown at the operator at rim speed.
  • The riving knife sits in the kerf right behind the blade, the same thickness as the plate and thinner than the teeth. It physically holds the cut open so it cannot close on the back of the blade.
  • Turn the guard off and start a cut: the kickback warning appears and stays. That warning is the entire reason the part exists.

7. Rip and crosscut are different jobs

  • Ripping runs with the grain, guided by the fence. The chip comes off as a long fibre, which is why a rip blade has few teeth and deep gullets to carry it.
  • Crosscutting severs the fibres across their length, guided by the miter gauge in the slot. Nothing has to be carried far, but every fibre has to be cut cleanly or it lifts as a splinter.
  • The fence and the miter gauge are never used together for a through cut: the offcut is trapped between a moving board and a fixed fence, and trapped offcuts are thrown. The operation switch here shows one or the other for exactly that reason.

8. Try this

  • Make it burn. Fit the 80T blade, choose MDF, and feed at 10 mm/s. Watch the cut face behind the blade go brown. Now speed the feed up mid-cut and watch exactly where the mark stops — the timber records what you did and when.
  • Make it tear. Fit the 24T blade, choose plywood, and feed at 140 mm/s. Then fit the 80T and repeat at the same feed. Same push, same timber, completely different edge.
  • Find the ceiling. Oak, 70 mm thick, blade wound right up, 80T blade, brisk feed. Read the power against the 1656 W the motor has, and listen to the arbor speed drop.
  • Prove the height rule. Hold everything still and take the blade from just proud of the board to fully up. The chip load does not move a thousandth — but watch the teeth-in-cut and power readings.
  • Cost of the bevel. Set the height to 98 mm and read the projection. Now tilt to 45° and read it again. Where did the 29 mm go?

9. What is simplified

  • The cutting force uses one specific-force figure per timber. Real wood varies with moisture, grain direction and where in the tree it came from, and cutting across the grain takes more than cutting along it.
  • The blade is treated as perfectly flat and true. A real one runs out a few hundredths, which is part of why a kerf is wider than the plate.
  • Kickback is reported as a risk rather than simulated. A lesson that ends with the workpiece leaving the screen teaches nothing the warning has not already said.
  • The motor droops smoothly under load. A real induction motor has a torque curve with a knee in it and will stall outright past the far side.
