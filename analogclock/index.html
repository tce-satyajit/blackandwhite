Analog Clock — A Pendulum, a Gear Train and the Physics of Timekeeping

Grades 8–10 · Time period and frequency, the simple pendulum, gear ratios, energy transfer and thermal expansion

1. Oscillation, time period and frequency

  • An oscillation is one complete to-and-fro motion. The time period T is how long one oscillation takes, in seconds. The frequency f is how many happen each second, in hertz, and the two are just reciprocals: f = 1/T.
  • Half an oscillation — one side to the other — is called a beat, and this clock is built so one beat is exactly one second. The reading at the top of the screen is that beat, measured.
  • Any clock at all, from this one to a quartz watch to an atomic clock, is the same two things bolted together: something that repeats, and something that counts the repeats. Here the pendulum repeats and the brass counts.
  • So the accuracy of a clock is never about the gears. It is entirely about how reliably the oscillator repeats.

2. The simple pendulum — and why it works

  • Pull the bob aside by an angle θ. Gravity pulls straight down with mg, the rod takes the part along its own length, and what is left over is a restoring force along the arc: F = −mg sin θ.
  • For a small angle, sin θ ≈ θ in radians. The restoring force is then proportional to the displacement and directed back towards the middle — which is the definition of simple harmonic motion.
  • Work the SHM through and the period comes out as T = 2π√(L/g).
  • Look hard at what is not in that formula. Not the mass of the bob. Not how far you pull it. Not what it is made of. Only the length and the strength of gravity. That is the whole reason a pendulum can be trusted to keep time and a swinging weight on a spring cannot.
  • Rearrange for the length that gives T = 2 s and you get 0.994 m. One second out, one second back. That is why this clock is the height it is — and why a grandfather clock is the height it is. The physics chose the furniture.

3. Counting the swings: gear ratios

  • Two meshing wheels must have teeth of the same size, so the teeth that go past per second are equal: N1 n1 = N2 n2, where N is the number of teeth and n the turns per minute. A wheel with more teeth turns more slowly, in exact proportion.
  • Each swing lets half a tooth of the escape wheel past. It has 30 teeth, so 60 swings — one minute — turn it exactly once, and the seconds hand ticks once per beat, six degrees at a time.
  • From there it is just division: 60/8 then 64/8, which multiply to 60. So the escape wheel turns 60 times for one turn of the centre arbor, and that arbor carries the minute hand — one turn an hour.
  • In front of the dial the motion work divides again: 30/10 × 32/8 = 12, giving the hour hand.
  • Multiply the lot: 60 × 60 × 12 = 43 200 beats per turn of the hour hand. The clock is a counting machine in base 60, made of brass.
  • Same rule as a bicycle’s gears, and the same trade: gearing down multiplies the turning effect and divides the speed by the same factor. You never get both.

4. Energy: where the push comes from

  • The pendulum by itself is a perfect energy swap: at the ends of the swing all the energy is gravitational potential, Ep = mgh; at the bottom it is all kinetic, Ek = ½mv². Left alone, it would swing for ever.
  • It is not left alone. Air resistance and friction at the suspension take a little energy out every swing and turn it into heat. Energy is conserved, but it is dissipated — spread out where no machine can get it back.
  • So the clock has to put energy back in. Winding the mainspring stores elastic potential energy; the train carries it up to the escapement; the escapement gives the pendulum a small push each beat, replacing exactly what friction stole. Wind → spring → wheels → push → heat.
  • The fusee — the brass cone with the chain wound round it — exists because a spring is strong when wound and weak when nearly down. The chain pulls on a small radius while the spring is strong and works its way out to a large one as the spring weakens, so the moment it delivers, τ = F × r, stays the same all week. Without it the push would fade day by day and the clock would slowly change rate.
  • Turn the spring set right down and the clock stops. Not because it runs out of energy, but because the push is too small to beat friction — the arc collapses below what the escapement needs.

5. Why a real clock goes wrong

  • Length. Since T ∝ √L, a small change gives ΔT/T = ½ ΔL/L — about 43 seconds a day for every millimetre. That is the rating nut under the bob, and it is the only adjustment the clock has.
  • Heat. Solids expand: ΔL = LαΔθ, with α = 19×10−6 K−1 for brass. Ten degrees warmer makes this rod 0.19 mm longer, and the clock loses about 8 seconds a day. Warm clocks run slow — which is why good regulators use a gridiron of brass and steel rods pulling opposite ways, so the two expansions cancel. Work it out with the numbers above and check it against the 43 s/day/mm figure.
  • Amplitude. The small-angle approximation was an approximation. For a real arc, T ≈ T0(1 + θ&sub0;²/16) — a wider swing takes slightly longer. This is circular error, and it is why the arc has to be held constant.
  • All three act on the same quantity — the period — so their effects simply add. Read the rate as a sum of three small errors and you can chase each one down separately.
  • This is a lesson about measurement in general: a systematic error (wrong length) shifts every reading the same way and can be corrected once; a random error cannot. Getting a clock right is entirely a hunt for systematic errors.

6. Try this

  • Show that amplitude hardly matters. Take the spring set from 20% to 180%. The arc nearly triples — but look how little the beat changes. Now move the rating nut by 1 mm and watch 43 seconds a day appear at once. Length is the variable; everything else is a rounding error.
  • Measure g. Read the beat off the screen, double it for the period, take the pendulum length from the slider, and work out g = 4π²L/T². How close is it to 9.81?
  • Check the gear train. Set Show to Mechanism — the dial, the case and the stand all disappear and only the working parts and the hands are left. Watch the seconds hand for exactly one beat: it moves one mark. Now predict how many beats the hour hand needs for one full turn, and check it against 43 200.
  • Summer and winter, on paper. A brass rod grows by LαΔθ. Work out how much this pendulum lengthens between 5 °C and 35 °C, turn that into seconds a day, then move the rating nut by the same amount and check your answer against the read-out.
  • Find the circular error. Note the beat at 20% spring set, then at 180%. The length has not changed by a thousandth of a millimetre — so where does the difference in the period come from?
  • Rate the clock. Leave it at ×900 and adjust the nut until the drift stops growing. How small an adjustment can you actually make, and what does that tell you about the precision of the instrument?

Assumptions, and what is left out

  • The arc is treated as settling almost at once to whatever the drive can sustain. A real pendulum takes many minutes to build up or die away.
  • The escapement is not simulated tooth by tooth. The unlocking, drop and impulse are drawn faithfully, but the arc comes from a simple energy balance rather than from the pallet geometry.
  • Only the first term of the circular-error series is used. The exact result is an elliptic integral.
  • The room is held at a steady temperature. A real rod grows and shrinks with the weather, and that is one of the largest errors a pendulum clock actually suffers.
  • The mainspring is left permanently wound. The fusee is drawn and does its job, but the clock is never allowed to run down.
  • Air drag, air pressure and the buoyancy of the bob shift the rate of a real regulator by seconds a day, and none of them are here.
  • The strike is a simplified count on the hour. The pin wheel, fly and hammer are real, but there is no rack or snail to make the count self-correcting.
  • Past ×60 the pendulum and escapement are drawn slowed down, because nothing on a screen can show four hundred beats a second. The counting underneath is still exact.
